Title: Diets and Body Composition
Source: ISSN Position Stand — Diets and Body Composition (Aragon et al., 2017)
URL: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y
DOI: 10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y
License: CC BY 4.0 (Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition)
Tags: fat loss; muscle gain; calorie balance; diet types; protein; adherence

Summary (evidence-based position of the International Society of Sports Nutrition):

1. There are many diet types and eating styles, with numerous subtypes under each major archetype.

2. All body-composition assessment methods (DEXA, BIA, skinfolds, etc.) have strengths and limitations; expect some measurement error.

3. Fat loss is driven by a sustained caloric deficit. Energy balance is the primary lever.

4. Muscle gain is driven by a sustained caloric surplus combined with resistance training; the size of the surplus affects the ratio of lean to fat gained.

5. A wide range of dietary approaches — from low-fat to low-carbohydrate/ketogenic and everything between — can be similarly effective for improving body composition when calories and protein are matched.

6. Increasing protein beyond standard athletic recommendations can improve body composition. Higher protein intakes (2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass, and in some cases >3 g/kg) help retain lean mass in lean, resistance-trained subjects during a deficit, amplifying protein's thermic, satiating, and muscle-sparing effects.

7. Acute manipulation of meal frequency shows no significant body-composition advantage when total intake is held constant.

8. Long-term diet success depends on adherence and on suppressing/circumventing compensatory factors such as adaptive thermogenesis (metabolic adaptation).

Practical takeaways:
- Pick the dietary style you can adhere to — that's the strongest predictor of success.
- Set calories for your goal (deficit to lose fat, modest surplus to gain muscle), keep protein high, and train.
- Expect metabolic adaptation when dieting; use diet breaks/refeeds and patience rather than ever-larger deficits.
